0. Soil basics you need first

The one-paragraph picture

Soil is a pile of grains with gaps between them. The gaps hold water and air. Almost every problem in this subject comes from those gaps: too many gaps means the soil is weak and squashy; water stuck in the gaps of clay means it settles slowly for years; water flowing through the gaps floods excavations. Ground improvement is the set of tricks to fix the gaps:

  • Squeeze out air → compaction (sections 1, 2)
  • Squeeze out water faster → vertical drains (section 3)
  • Fill or glue the gaps → grouting, cement / lime, mechanical mixing (4, 5, 6)
  • Pump the water away → dewatering (7)

Deriving the two key formulas (2 lines)

Take Vs = 1. Then voids = e, total V = 1 + e, solids weigh Gγw, water weighs wGγw so its volume is wG.

Saturation = water volume / void volume: S=wGe⇒Se=wG

Dry unit weight = solids weight / total volume: γd=Gγw1+e

Fewer gaps (smaller e) means higher γd. That's all compaction is.

Grain sizes and D-values

  • Gravel > 4.75 mm · sand 0.075-4.75 mm · silt 0.002-0.075 mm · clay < 0.002 mm (75 µ = 0.075 mm is the "fines" cut-off)
  • D10 = the size that 10% of the soil (by weight) is finer than. D50 = median size. A small D10 means the finest tenth of the soil is very fine: if D10 < 0.075 mm, at least 10% of the soil is fines (silt / clay). It does not mean the soil is mostly fines.
  • Cohesionless (sand, gravel): grains don't stick, strength from friction, drains fast. Cohesive (clay): tiny plate-shaped, electrically charged particles that stick together, hold water, drain very slowly.
  • Plasticity index PI = LL − PL: the range of water content over which a soil is mouldable like putty. High PI = very clayey, swells and shrinks a lot. PI = 0 means non-plastic (sand).

Permeability and consolidation

  • Permeability k = how easily water flows through soil (Darcy: v = k·i). Gravel ≈ 10−2 m/s, sand ≈ 10−4, silt ≈ 10−6, clay ≈ 10−9 m/s. Every method choice in dewatering and grouting is really a choice based on k.
  • Consolidation: load a saturated clay and the grains can't move closer until the water between them leaves. Water is incompressible, so at first it carries the load (excess pore pressure). Since clay's k is tiny, the water leaks out over months or years, and the ground settles slowly.
  • The key fact: consolidation time grows with the square of the distance water must travel (t ∝ H²/cv). Halve the drainage path → 4× faster. Vertical drains exploit exactly this.
  • Compaction ≠ consolidation. Compaction removes air instantly with machines; consolidation removes water slowly under sustained load.

1. Compaction (lab)

Understand first

Compaction = pounding or rolling soil so grains pack closer and air is pushed out. Denser soil is stronger, settles less and lets less water through.

Why does water content matter, and why is there a peak? Take a dry clayey soil and compact it: grains grip each other through friction and suction, so they don't slide into the gaps. Add a little water and it acts like a lubricant, grains slide into denser packing, so γd rises. Keep adding water and eventually the gaps are mostly water, which the rammer can't squeeze out, and water now takes space the grains could have used, so γd falls. The peak is the maximum dry density (MDD) at the optimum moisture content (OMC).

ZAV line: the best you could ever do at a given w is remove every bit of air (S = 1). Put S = 1 into Se = wG → e = wG, then into γd = Gγw/(1+e) and you get the ZAV formula. Real curves always sit below it because some air stays trapped.

More energy (Modified Proctor, heavier roller) pushes the curve up and to the left: denser, and the peak happens at a lower water content because less lubrication is needed when you hit harder.

Dry vs wet side is about particle arrangement in clay. Dry of OMC, there's too little water for plates to slide, so they lock edge-to-face like a house of cards (flocculated): big channels, so more permeable; stiff and strong but brittle; can suck in water later and swell. Wet of OMC, plates line up parallel (dispersed): tight, low permeability, soft and flexible, less swelling but more shrinkage. Every row in the table below follows from that one picture.

2. Field compaction and rollers

Understand first

In the field you can't use a rammer, so you use rollers. The rule: match the mechanism to how the soil resists packing.

  • Clays stick together in lumps. They need high pressure and kneading to break and remould lumps → sheepsfoot (spikes concentrate weight onto small areas) and pneumatic rollers.
  • Sands and gravels don't stick, they're held loose by friction. Shaking them lets grains drop into gaps → vibratory rollers and plates. Static pressure alone barely works on them.
  • Lumpy or rocky fill → grid roller crushes it. Deep loose fill → impact roller drops a heavy many-sided drum like a hammer.

Roller output formula, built up: width × speed = area rolled per hour. × layer thickness = volume per hour. But each strip needs N passes, so ÷ N. × 1000 converts km/h into m/h, and × E allows for turning, overlaps and stops (efficiency, ~0.75).

  • Lift thickness (typically 150-300 mm loose)
  • Number of passes and roller speed
  • Contact pressure / roller weight and type matched to soil
  • Soil type and gradation; removal of oversize and organics

Field control methods

  • Density: sand replacement, core cutter (soft fine soils), rubber balloon, water displacement, nuclear densometer
  • Compare with lab MDD → relative compaction; check w within OMC ± 2%

3. Vertical drains

Understand first

Problem: you want to build on a 10 m thick soft clay. Load it and it will settle, but because water has to leak 10 m vertically through clay, that takes years. You can't wait that long.

Fix: push columns of sand (or plastic strip drains, PVDs) into the clay every ~2 m. Now water only travels about 1 m sideways into the nearest drain, then rises up the drain easily. Since time ∝ (distance)², going from 10 m to 1 m makes it roughly 100× faster. Bonus: clay is usually layered, so it is more permeable horizontally (kh > kv), which helps radial flow. Then you preload (pile extra fill on top) to force the settlement out before building.

Reading the formulas, one by one:

  • de: each drain "owns" a patch of soil around it (a square or a hexagon). Barron's math needs a circle, so we use a circle of the same area. Square: s² = πde²/4 → de = 1.128s. Hexagon: area 0.866s² → de = 1.05s.
  • dw: a PVD is a flat strip, not a circle. Treat it as a circle with the same perimeter: πdw = 2(a+b).
  • n = de/dw: how big the soil patch is compared to the drain. Bigger n = farther to travel = slower.
  • Tr: "time factor", a dimensionless clock. Faster-draining soil (ch) and more time push it up; a bigger patch (de²) pulls it down. Same idea as Tv for vertical flow, with H replaced by de.
  • m (or F(n)): a geometry penalty from Barron's solution; grows roughly like ln n. Just plug in.
  • Ur = 1 − e−8Tr/m: consolidation creeps towards 100% exponentially. U = fraction of settlement already done.
  • Combining: work with the fraction not yet done. If vertical flow alone leaves 84% undone and radial alone leaves 18.5% undone, both together leave 0.84 × 0.185 = 15.5% undone → U = 84.5%.
  • ch/cv = kh/kv: c = k/(mvγw) and mv is the same in both directions, so the ratio of c equals the ratio of k. That's how A3 Q5 gets ch = 4.5cv.
  • Smear: driving the mandrel remoulds a ring of clay around the drain, lowering kh there and slowing things. Both assignment questions say ignore it.

Sand drains: installation

  • Displacement: closed-end mandrel driven or vibrated, filled with sand, withdrawn. Fast but causes smear and disturbance.
  • Non-displacement: jetting, rotary or continuous-flight auger, hole filled with sand. Less disturbance.
  • Sandwicks: geotextile sock filled with sand (≈ 65 mm), avoids necking. PVDs: band drain (core + filter) pushed in by mandrel, anchor plate at tip.
  • Sand blanket on top, then preload / surcharge; monitor with piezometers and settlement plates.

Design parameters

  • Drain diameter and type; spacing and pattern (n = de/dw)
  • Length / depth of the clay layer, drainage boundaries
  • ch, cv, kh/kv; required U and time available
  • Smear zone and well resistance; filter criteria; sand blanket thickness; surcharge magnitude and stages

Advantages / disadvantages

  • + Large cut in consolidation time, earlier strength gain, cheap per m, PVDs fast to install
  • − Smear and disturbance, well resistance, drains can kink or neck under large settlement, needs surcharge and time, useless in very thin or organic layers with high secondary compression

4. Grouting

Understand first

Grouting = drilling holes and pumping a liquid into the ground that later sets hard. Purpose: make soil or rock stronger and/or watertight (stop seepage under dams, into tunnels, excavations).

There are three ways the grout can act:

  • Permeation: grout flows into the gaps between grains without moving them, like water soaking into sand, then sets. Only works if the gaps are big enough for the grout.
  • Compaction grouting: very stiff grout is forced in as a growing bulb that shoves the surrounding loose soil aside, densifying it.
  • Jet grouting: a high-pressure jet cuts up the soil and mixes it with cement as the rod rotates and rises, making a column of "soilcrete". Works in any soil because it doesn't rely on gaps.

Groutability ratio: cement grout is a suspension of real particles (~0.01-0.1 mm). The pore openings in soil are roughly D15/5 wide. Put the soil's fine end (D15) over the grout's coarse end (D85): if the ratio is > 25 the biggest grout particles slip through; below 11 they clog the surface. That's why cement grout only works in gravels and coarse sand, while chemical grouts (true solutions, no particles, e.g. sodium silicate) reach fine sands and coarse silts.

Why stages? A long hole can't be grouted in one shot: grout would rush out of the weakest zone and pressure near the surface would heave the ground. So the hole is grouted in 3-6 m lengths (stages), isolated by a packer (an inflatable seal). Downstage works from the top so the top is sealed before you use high pressure deeper; upstage drills once and works upward for speed.

Groutability ratio (soil vs grout particle size) N1=D15,soilD85,groutN2=D10,soilD95,grout N1 > 25 groutable, < 11 not · N2 > 11 groutable, < 6 not

Characteristics of grouts

  • Rheology / viscosity: decides penetration; chemicals are near water-thin, cement is a suspension
  • Particle size: must pass the soil pores (groutability ratio)
  • Setting / gel time: controllable, long enough to place, short enough to stay put
  • Stability: low bleeding and segregation (bentonite added to cement grout)
  • Strength and permanence / durability after setting; resistance to washout and chemical attack
  • Toxicity and cost

Types (one line each)

  • Permeation: fills voids without disturbing soil (gravels, sands)
  • Compaction: stiff grout bulb displaces and densifies loose soil
  • Jet: high-pressure jet erodes and mixes soil into soilcrete columns (all soils)
  • Fracture / hydrofracture: grout lenses split the soil, used for heave and settlement control

Curtain vs blanket (A5 Q2, Q4)

  • Curtain grouting is the answer to "vertical seepage barrier beneath hydraulic structures": one to three rows of deep holes at the upstream heel, grouted to form an impervious vertical screen. Reduces seepage and uplift under dams and weirs.
  • Blanket (consolidation) grouting: shallow (5-15 m), closely spaced holes on a grid over the whole foundation area. Seals and stiffens the fractured top rock, improves bearing, and gives a cap so curtain grout can be pumped at higher pressure.
  • Order on site: blanket first, then curtain.

5. Cement and lime stabilisation

Understand first

Cement works like making very weak concrete in place: mix cement into soil, compact, cure. Cement hydrates and glues grains together. Works best where there are grains to glue (granular soils); in fat clays it's hard to mix and the clay coats the cement.

Lime works mainly by changing clay chemistry. Why clay is troublesome: clay plates carry negative charge and hold a cloud of water and cations around them (the "double layer"). With sodium (Na+, single charge) the cloud is thick: the clay swells, is very plastic, and sticky. Lime supplies Ca2+, which has double the charge and neutralises the plates with a thinner cloud. Plates then clump together (flocculate), so the soil turns crumbly and workable, the PI drops, and swelling reduces. Over months, lime also reacts with the silica and alumina in the clay itself (pozzolanic reaction) to form cementing gels, so strength keeps growing.

So: lime needs clay to react with; sand has nothing for lime to work on → use cement there.

Factors affecting cement stabilisation

  • Soil type: best for well-graded granular soils; high-plasticity clays hard to mix, need more cement
  • Cement content: strength rises with it (typically 5-14% by weight)
  • Water content (near OMC for compaction and hydration)
  • Mixing: thoroughness and delay between mixing and compaction (delay lowers strength)
  • Compaction density achieved
  • Curing: moisture and temperature, curing period
  • Deleterious matter: organics and sulphates retard or attack hydration
  • Additives (lime, fly ash, calcium chloride)

Mechanism of lime stabilisation

  1. Cation exchange: Ca2+ replaces Na+, K+ on clay surfaces, shrinking the diffuse double layer
  2. Flocculation and agglomeration: particles clump, soil turns friable; PI drops, workability up (immediate)
  3. Carbonation: lime + CO2 → CaCO3, weak cementing
  4. Pozzolanic reaction (long term): Ca(OH)2 + clay silica / alumina → CSH and CAH gels that cement particles; strength keeps rising for months

Most effective for clays (medium to high plasticity, PI > 10, e.g. black cotton soil): reduces swelling and PI. Poor for sands and silts that have no reactive clay minerals. Usual dose 2-8%.

6. Mechanical stabilisation

Understand first

No chemicals: just blend two natural soils so the mix is better than either. Think of concrete: gravel and sand (aggregate) form a strong, frictional skeleton; a little clay (binder) fills the gaps and holds it together, like cement paste. Too little binder: loose and ravels. Too much: the gravel floats in clay and the mix becomes weak and sensitive to water.

Fuller's curve is the grading that packs densest (small particles exactly filling the gaps of larger ones).

PI of a mix, why that formula: plasticity comes only from the fine fraction (passing 425 µ). So each material's PI is weighted by how much fine material it actually adds: its share in the mix (X) times its % passing 425 µ (S). It's a weighted average of PI over the fines. A PI of 0 is replaced by 1 by convention (the prof's slide rule), otherwise the material would drop out of the numerator entirely.

Proportioning formula: mixing is linear. If a fraction x of B is mixed with (1−x) of A, % passing = A + x(B − A). Set it equal to the target E and solve: x = (E − A)/(B − A).

7. Dewatering

Understand first

Dig below the water table and water pours in, slopes slump, and in fine sands the base can "boil" (quick condition: upward flow lifts the grains). So you lower the water table first, around and below the excavation. The whole topic is: how easily does water flow out of this soil? (permeability k). Read the method table from top to bottom as k gets smaller:

  • Gravel / coarse sand: water flows freely, so just let it collect in a sump and pump it.
  • Sands: ring the excavation with small filtered pipes (well points) connected to a suction pump. Suction can only lift water about 5-6 m in practice (atmospheric pressure limits it to ~10 m in theory), so for deeper digs you add another ring lower down: multistage. Or use deep wells with submersible pumps that push rather than suck.
  • Silty fine sands: water is held by capillary tension and won't drain by gravity alone, so seal the well point and apply vacuum to increase the pull (gradient).
  • Silts and clays: k is so tiny that no practical gradient works. Electro-osmosis: clay particles are negatively charged, so the water next to them is full of positive ions. Apply DC current: the positive ions migrate to the negative electrode (cathode) and drag the water with them. Make the cathode a well point and pump the water away. The electro-osmotic ke is roughly the same for all soils, unlike the ordinary k, which is why it beats ordinary drainage in clay.

Sichardt R tells you how far out the lowered water table spreads: more drawdown and more permeable soil → wider cone. Matters because neighbouring buildings inside that cone can settle.

Vacuum well points (A4 Q3)

  • For silty fine sands where gravity flow is too slow.
  • Installation: jet a hole, place well point and riser, fill around it with a coarse sand filter, then seal the top 1-1.5 m with clay or bentonite so a vacuum holds in the filter. Pump keeps the header under vacuum.
  • Vacuum adds to the hydraulic gradient, pulling water from fine soils.

Field control parameters (A4 Q6)

  • Groundwater level and drawdown (piezometers / observation wells)
  • Discharge rate and fines (sand) in discharge: signals piping or filter failure
  • Settlement of the site and neighbouring structures
  • Soil permeability, aquifer thickness, radius of influence
  • Pump vacuum and performance, standby power and pumps
  • Disposal of pumped water; uplift on the excavation base

Assignment 1: Compaction

A1·1Explain suitability number. Find SN for backfill with D50 = 1 mm, D20 = 0.5 mm, D10 = 0.08 mm and rate it.

Suitability number (Brown, 1977) rates a granular soil as backfill for vibroflotation. Backfill is poured down beside the vibrator and must sink fast and drain freely, so coarse, clean sand is ideal and fines are harmful.

SN = 1.7 √(3/D50² + 1/D20² + 1/D10²), D in mm. Lower SN = better.

Rating: 0-10 excellent, 10-20 good, 20-30 fair, 30-50 poor, > 50 unsuitable.

Calculation: 3/1 + 1/0.25 + 1/0.0064 = 3 + 4 + 156.25 = 163.25. √163.25 = 12.78. SN = 1.7 × 12.78 = 21.7 → fair backfill.

A1·2Discuss the properties of soil compacted dry of optimum and wet of optimum.

The difference comes from soil structure: dry of OMC, clay plates lack water to slide, so they lock in a random edge-to-face (flocculated) arrangement; wet of OMC they align parallel (dispersed).

  • Permeability: dry side higher (open flocculated pores); wet side lower
  • Shear strength: dry side higher but brittle; wet side lower, ductile
  • Swelling: dry side swells more (water-deficient, sucks water in); wet side less
  • Shrinkage: dry side less; wet side more
  • Construction pore pressure: dry side lower; wet side higher
  • Compressibility: dry side lower at low stress (but structure collapses at high stress); wet side higher at low stress
  • Stress-strain: dry side stiff, high modulus; wet side flexible, tolerates deformation without cracking

A1·3How will you control compaction in the field?
  • Before: lab Proctor test on the borrow soil gives MDD and OMC; specify relative compaction (usually ≥ 95% of MDD) and moisture range (OMC ± 2%)
  • Placement: spread in uniform loose lifts (150-300 mm), remove oversize and organic matter
  • Moisture adjustment: sprinkle water if dry, aerate / scarify if wet; check w by calcium carbide meter, sand bath, alcohol burning, Proctor needle, nuclear gauge
  • Rolling: suitable roller for the soil, fixed number of passes established from a trial strip
  • Density check: sand replacement, core cutter, rubber balloon, water displacement or nuclear method at specified frequency
  • Compute γd = γ/(1+w) and RC = γd,field/γd,max × 100; if below spec, re-roll or rework the layer before placing the next
A1·4What is the effect of compaction on engineering properties? How do you decide whether to compact dry or wet of optimum?
  • Density and shear strength increase; bearing capacity increases
  • Permeability decreases
  • Compressibility and settlement decrease
  • Stability of slopes and embankments improves
  • Swelling may increase (dry side), shrinkage may increase (wet side)
  • Liquefaction potential of sands reduces

Decision depends on what the structure needs:

  • Dry of optimum where strength and stiffness matter: pavement subgrades, foundations, retaining wall backfill (strength + free drainage)
  • Wet of optimum where watertightness and flexibility matter: dam cores, canal and landfill liners (low k, tolerates settlement without cracking); also for expansive clays to reduce later swelling
A1·5What are the factors that affect compaction in the laboratory?
  • Water content: lubricates particles up to OMC, then occupies space
  • Compactive effort: more energy → higher MDD at lower OMC
  • Soil type: well-graded coarse soils reach high MDD at low OMC; clays give low MDD, high OMC, flat curves; uniform sands show little peak
  • Method of compaction: impact, kneading, static or vibratory give different curves for the same energy
  • Admixtures: lime, cement, salts change OMC and MDD
  • Recompaction: reusing the same sample crushes particles and gives higher densities, so fresh samples are preferred
  • Mould size / layer thickness
A1·6What is the zero air voids line?

The curve of dry density vs water content for the soil with no air at all (S = 100%): γd = Gγw/(1 + wG). It is the theoretical maximum dry density at each water content. Compaction cannot expel all air, so the compaction curve always lies to the left of and below it, approaching it on the wet side. Lines for 5% and 10% air voids, γd = (1 − na)Gγw/(1 + wG), are drawn parallel to it. Used to check that test results are physically possible.

A1·7Describe the Standard and Modified Proctor tests. How do you decide which test to conduct?

Procedure (common): take about 3 kg of soil passing 4.75 mm, add water, compact in the 1000 ml mould in layers with the rammer, trim, weigh, take a water content sample. Repeat at 4-6 increasing water contents. Plot γd vs w; the peak gives MDD and OMC; draw the ZAV line.

  • Standard (IS 2720 Part 7): 2.6 kg rammer, 310 mm fall, 3 layers × 25 blows, about 600 kJ/m³
  • Modified (IS 2720 Part 8): 4.89 kg rammer, 450 mm fall, 5 layers × 25 blows, about 2700 kJ/m³ (4.5 times)

Choice: match the field energy. Modified for heavy loads and heavy compaction plant (airfield pavements, highways, heavy embankments); Standard for ordinary fills and light equipment.

A1·8Embankment of 106 m³ at e = 0.80, borrow pits A, B, C with e = 0.90, 1.50, 1.80 and cost 0.25, 0.23, 0.18 Rs/m³. Which pit is most economical?

Solids volume is constant: Vs = 106/1.80 = 5.556 × 105 m³.

  • A: 1.90 Vs = 10.56 × 105 m³ → Rs 2.64 lakh
  • B: 2.50 Vs = 13.89 × 105 m³ → Rs 3.19 lakh
  • C: 2.80 Vs = 15.56 × 105 m³ → Rs 2.80 lakh

Pit A is most economical even though its rate is highest, because it needs the least excavation.

A1·9Compaction test data given (mould 1000 cc, 1000 g, G = 2.70). Find OMC, MDD, ZAV line and S at MDD.

γ (g/cc) = (total − 1000)/1000: 1.925, 2.095, 2.150, 2.125, 2.070. γd = γ/(1+w): 1.750, 1.871, 1.881, 1.830, 1.751.

OMC ≈ 14%, MDD ≈ 1.88 g/cc. ZAV: γd = 2.7/(1 + 2.7w) → 2.04 (12%), 1.96 (14%), 1.89 (16%), 1.82 (18%). At MDD, e = 2.70/1.881 − 1 = 0.435, S = 0.143 × 2.70/0.435 = 88.7%. See the plotted curve in section 1.

Assignment 2: Field compaction

A2·1What types of compactors are recommended for different types of soil?
  • Smooth wheel roller: gravel, sand, crushed rock, well-graded sand and low-plasticity silt; not uniform sand or soft clay
  • Sheepsfoot / padfoot (tamping) roller: cohesive soils, silty and clayey soils
  • Pneumatic tyred roller: both cohesive and cohesionless soils (most versatile)
  • Grid roller: weathered rock, well-graded coarse soils
  • Vibratory roller / vibratory plate: cohesionless granular soils
  • Rammers and tampers: all soils, confined areas
  • Impact roller: loose fills and deep layers
A2·2How would you estimate the output of a roller?

Output (compacted m³/h) = W × S × L × 1000 × E / N

  • W = effective rolling width (m), allowing for overlap
  • S = rolling speed (km/h), × 1000 to get m/h
  • L = compacted lift thickness (m)
  • N = number of passes needed (from a trial strip)
  • E = job efficiency (about 0.75 for turning, delays)

Logic: width × speed = area per hour; × thickness = volume per hour; each strip needs N passes so divide by N.

A2·3How does a sheepsfoot roller compact the earth?
  • Steel drum with rows of projecting feet / lugs (17.5-23 cm long); drum ballasted with water or wet sand; weight 50-80 kN; speed 6-10 km/h
  • Only the feet touch the soil, so weight is concentrated on a small area: very high contact pressure
  • Feet penetrate the loose lift and compact it from the bottom up with pressure plus kneading, breaking up clods
  • As the layer densifies, the feet penetrate less and the drum rises: rolling continues until the feet no longer sink in fully, called walk-out
  • Compacts layers of about 30 cm; best for cohesive soils; leaves the surface loose and dimpled, which helps bonding with the next lift

A2·4What factors must be kept in mind for desired compaction in the field?
  • Water content near OMC
  • Lift (layer) thickness
  • Number of passes
  • Type of roller matched to soil
  • Contact pressure / weight of roller (static pressure = weight / contact length)
  • Rolling speed
  • Soil type and gradation, removal of oversize and organics
  • Uniform coverage and overlap; drainage of the area during work
A2·5What methods are adopted for measuring water content and density in the field?

Water content: oven drying (reference, slow), calcium carbide / rapid moisture meter (CaC2 + water → acetylene gas pressure), sand bath, alcohol burning, Proctor needle (penetration resistance vs w calibration), nuclear moisture gauge.

Density: sand replacement (granular / gravelly soils), core cutter (soft fine soils, no gravel), rubber balloon, water displacement (cohesive lumps), nuclear densometer (fast, non-destructive).

A2·6Which rollers suit (i) cohesive soils and (ii) cohesionless soils?

(i) Cohesive: sheepsfoot, padfoot / tamping and pneumatic tyred rollers: they deliver high pressure and kneading that breaks lumps and remoulds the clay. Vibration does little because clay particles are bonded and the water can't escape quickly.

(ii) Cohesionless: vibratory rollers and vibratory plates (high frequency, low amplitude shaking lets grains settle into a denser packing), smooth wheel rollers for finishing, pneumatic rollers. Sheepsfoot is useless here: feet just shove the sand aside.

A2·7What are grid rollers? For which soil are they used?

A roller whose drum is a heavy steel grid / mesh instead of a solid surface. Weight about 5 t, 15 t ballasted, speed 5-24 km/h. The grid concentrates pressure, crushes oversize particles and breaks clods, pushing fines down. Used for weathered rocks and well-graded coarse soils, in road subgrades and sub-bases.

A2·8What are impact rollers? For which soil are they most suitable?

Rollers with a non-circular (pentagonal) drum. As it rotates, the mass is lifted and falls on each face, delivering impact blows (10-25 kJ) continuously, like dynamic compaction on wheels, combined with static pressure and kneading. Reaches much greater depth (1-3 m) than ordinary rollers, breaks lumps, levels ruts, and works over a wider range of moisture contents. Most suitable for loose fills, collapsible and dry sandy soils, and thick layers, for example large earthworks and proof-rolling.

Assignment 3: Vertical drains

A3·1What are sand drains? How are they installed? How is the average degree of consolidation estimated?

Vertical columns of sand (200-600 mm dia, 1.5-6 m c/c) installed through soft saturated clay to shorten the drainage path. Water flows radially to the drains and up to a sand blanket, so a preload / surcharge consolidates the clay in months instead of years.

Installation: displacement method: closed-end mandrel driven / vibrated, filled with sand, withdrawn (fast, causes smear). Non-displacement: jetting, rotary drilling or continuous-flight auger, then sand filling (less disturbance). Sand blanket placed on top, then surcharge; monitored with piezometers and settlement plates.

Average degree: radial: Ur = 1 − exp(−8Tr/m), Tr = cht/de², m = F(n), n = de/dw. Vertical: Uv from Tv = cvt/Hd². Combined (Carrillo): (1 − U) = (1 − Uv)(1 − Ur).

Draw the sand drain section from section 3.

A3·2What are the various parameters for the design of sand drains?
  • Diameter of drain dw (or equivalent diameter of PVD)
  • Spacing s and pattern (square / triangular) → de = 1.13s or 1.05s
  • Length / depth of penetration through the compressible layer
  • Soil properties: ch, cv, kh/kv, compressibility
  • Required degree of consolidation and time available
  • Smear zone (reduced k around drain) and well resistance of the drain
  • Filter / sand gradation, sand blanket thickness (about 0.3-1 m)
  • Magnitude and staging of surcharge load
A3·3What are the advantages and disadvantages of vertical drains?

Advantages:

  • Drastically shortens consolidation time (path from H to about s/2; time ∝ path²)
  • Uses higher horizontal permeability
  • Faster strength gain, so embankments can be raised faster and more safely
  • Reduces post-construction settlement
  • PVDs are cheap, quick to install, uniform quality

Disadvantages:

  • Smear and disturbance reduce k near the drain
  • Well resistance; sand drains may neck or be discontinuous, PVDs can kink under large settlement
  • Need surcharge and waiting time anyway
  • Ineffective for secondary compression (organic soils, peat)
  • Displacement installation can disturb sensitive clays
A3·4PVDs 100 × 4 mm, square 2 m, clay 10 m, cv = 2 and ch = 3 m²/yr, impervious base. U after 1 year, no smear?

dw = 2(100+4)/π = 66.2 mm; de = 2.26 m; n = 34.1; m = 2.78. Tr = 0.587 → Ur = 81.5%. Hd = 10 m, Tv = 0.02 → Uv = 16%. U = 1 − 0.84 × 0.185 = 84.5%. Full steps in section 3.

A3·5Sandwicks 10 cm dia, triangular 2 m c/c, cv = 3 × 10−3 cm²/s, kh = 4.5kv. Time for 90% radial consolidation?

de = 2.10 m, n = 21, m = 2.30, ch = 1.35 × 10−6 m²/s. Tr = 2.303 × 2.30/8 = 0.663. t = 0.663 × 4.41/1.35 × 10−6 = 2.16 × 106 s ≈ 25 days.

Assignment 4: Mechanical stabilisation and dewatering

A4·1Road material (PI 0) + binder (PI 13). Binder needed for 45 kg of road material to get PI = 6?

PImix = (X S1P1 + Y S2P2)/(X S1 + Y S2), S = % passing 425 µ, PI 0 → use 1. S1 = 25, S2 = 85: 6 = (25X + 1105Y)/(25X + 85Y) → Y/X = 125/595 = 0.21. Binder = 0.21 × 45 = 9.45 kg.

A4·2What are well points? Why is a multistage well point system required? Explain with sketch.

Well point: a small-diameter (50-75 mm) perforated pipe, about 1 m long, with a filter screen, jetted into the ground. Many are installed in a line around the excavation at 0.75-2 m spacing, each connected by a riser and swing pipe to a common header pipe and a combined vacuum + centrifugal pump. Suitable for sands and sandy gravels.

Multistage: the pump lifts water by suction, and atmospheric pressure limits suction lift; after losses a single stage can lower the water table only about 5-6 m. For deeper excavations a second ring is installed on a berm lower down (and a third if needed), each stage giving about 4.5-6 m more drawdown.

Draw single and multistage sketches from section 7.

A4·3What are vacuum well points? For which soils are they most effective? How are they installed?

Well points in which a vacuum is maintained in the filter zone, adding to the hydraulic gradient driving water to the well point. Effective in silty fine sands and sandy silts (k ≈ 10−5 to 10−7 m/s) where water is held by capillarity and won't drain by gravity.

Installation: jet a hole (about 250-300 mm) with a casing, insert the well point and riser, fill the annulus with coarse sand filter up to near the top, seal the top 1-1.5 m with clay / bentonite so the vacuum is held, connect to header and vacuum pump. Spacing closer than ordinary well points.

A4·4Which dewatering technique is most suitable for cohesive soil? Explain with sketch.

Electro-osmosis. Clays and silts have permeability below about 10−7 m/s, so gravity, suction or vacuum drainage are too slow. A DC field moves water regardless of the low hydraulic permeability (see A4·5 and the sketch in section 7).

A4·5For which soils is electro-osmosis used? Explain with sketch.

Used for silts, silty clays and clays (very low k). Clay particles carry negative surface charge, so pore water near them contains excess positive cations. When DC voltage (about 50-100 V) is applied between anodes (steel rods) and cathodes (well points), the cations migrate to the cathode and drag pore water with them. Water collecting at the cathode is pumped out.

  • Flow velocity v = ke × (E/L); ke ≈ 5 × 10−5 cm²/s per V, nearly the same for all soils
  • Also reduces pore pressure and increases shear strength (stabilises slopes, excavations)
  • Drawbacks: high power cost, corrosion of anodes
A4·6What field control parameters should be considered while designing a dewatering system?
  • Soil profile, permeability and aquifer thickness (pumping tests)
  • Initial water table and required drawdown below excavation base
  • Radius of influence (Sichardt R = 3000 s√k) and effect on nearby structures
  • Rate of discharge and pump capacity, with standby pumps and power
  • Piezometric levels monitored during pumping
  • Fines content in discharge (sign of piping / filter failure)
  • Settlement of adjacent ground and buildings
  • Bottom heave / uplift and slope stability
  • Disposal of discharged water; duration of dewatering

Assignment 5: Grouting and chemical stabilisation

A5·1Explain ascending and descending stage grouting with sketches; advantages and disadvantages.

Descending (downstage): drill the first stage (3-6 m), grout it, let it set, re-drill through the set grout to the next stage, grout with packer seated at the top of that stage, repeat downward.

Ascending (upstage): drill the full depth at once, set the packer above the bottom stage and grout it, raise the packer one stage, grout, repeat upward.

Descending + upper zone sealed first so higher pressures can be used below without surface leakage, hole stable, each stage tested. − Slow and costly (repeated drilling, setting time).

Ascending + faster, cheaper, one drilling operation, continuous. − Needs a stable hole that won't collapse; grout can bypass the packer through fissures; limited pressure near the surface.

A5·2Which grouting method is widely used for making vertical seepage barriers beneath hydraulic structures? Explain with sketch.

Curtain grouting. One to three rows of closely spaced, deep holes (often down to 0.3-0.7 of the reservoir head, or to an impervious stratum) along the upstream heel of a dam or weir. Holes are grouted in stages using split-spacing (primary holes first, then secondary and tertiary in between) until the grout take is low. The grouted zone forms a continuous, nearly impervious vertical screen that cuts seepage and reduces uplift under the structure. Blanket grouting of the top rock is normally done first so the curtain can be grouted at higher pressure. Draw the dam sketch from section 4.

A5·3Explain the characteristics of grouts.
  • Rheology / viscosity: governs penetration and pumping pressure; Newtonian chemical solutions penetrate finer pores than Bingham cement suspensions
  • Particle size: must satisfy groutability ratio (D15 soil/D85 grout > 25)
  • Setting / gel time: must be controllable
  • Stability: low bleeding and sedimentation; bentonite added to cement grout
  • Strength after setting
  • Permanence / durability: resistance to leaching, washout, chemical attack, creep
  • Toxicity and environmental safety
  • Cost

Types: suspensions (cement, clay, cement-bentonite), solutions / chemical (sodium silicate, resins), emulsions (bitumen).

A5·5What factors affect cement stabilisation?
  • Soil type (best for well-graded granular, poor for high-plasticity clays and organics)
  • Cement content (5-14%)
  • Moisture content and compaction density
  • Mixing efficiency and delay between mixing and compaction
  • Curing conditions and period
  • Presence of organic matter and sulphates
  • Additives (lime, fly ash, CaCl2)
  • Freeze-thaw and wet-dry durability requirements
A5·6Explain the mechanism of lime stabilisation. For which soils is it most effective?
  1. Cation exchange: Ca2+ from lime replaces Na+, K+ on clay surfaces; diffuse double layer shrinks
  2. Flocculation and agglomeration: particles clump; PI falls, workability and texture improve (immediate, hours)
  3. Carbonation: Ca(OH)2 + CO2 → CaCO3, weak cementation
  4. Pozzolanic reaction: Ca(OH)2 + clay silica and alumina → calcium silicate and aluminate hydrates (CSH, CAH) that bind particles; long-term strength gain over months

Most effective for fine-grained clayey soils (PI > 10, e.g. expansive black cotton soil): reduces plasticity, swelling and shrinkage, raises strength and CBR. Not effective for sands and non-plastic silts that lack reactive clay minerals. Usual dose 2-8%.

Likely extra questions

X·1What is ground improvement? State objectives and classify methods.

Modifying the in-situ properties of weak ground to meet project needs when replacing it or using deep foundations is costly.

Objectives: increase shear strength and bearing capacity, reduce compressibility and settlement, reduce or increase permeability, prevent liquefaction, control swelling.

Classification: mechanical (compaction, vibroflotation, dynamic compaction), hydraulic (preloading, vertical drains, dewatering, electro-osmosis), chemical / physical (grouting, cement, lime, mechanical blending), inclusions (stone columns, geosynthetics).

X·3What is preloading? Why is it combined with vertical drains?

Placing a temporary load (fill, sometimes more than the design load = surcharge) on soft clay before construction so primary consolidation happens in advance; the load is then removed and the structure built with little remaining settlement. Alone it is too slow for thick clays because time ∝ H². Vertical drains shorten the drainage path so the preload achieves the settlement within the construction schedule; staged loading also lets clay gain strength to avoid failure.

X·4What is the groutability ratio? Why can't cement grout be used in fine sands?

N = D15 (soil) / D85 (grout); > 25 groutable, < 11 not. Also D10/D95: > 11 groutable, < 6 not. Cement particles (up to about 0.1 mm) are larger than the pore throats of fine sands, so they filter out at the hole wall and form a cake instead of penetrating. Chemical grouts (true solutions) or microfine cement are used for finer soils.

X·5What is smear effect and well resistance in vertical drains?

Smear: installing a drain with a mandrel remoulds a ring of clay around it (about 2-3 times the mandrel size), lowering kh there and slowing radial consolidation. Well resistance: the drain itself has finite discharge capacity, so with long drains or kinked / silted PVDs, water flow up the drain is restricted. Both make actual consolidation slower than Barron's ideal solution.

X·6Write short notes on rammers and vibratory plate compactors.

Rammers: impact compaction for small, confined areas (trenches, behind walls); hand type 3-5 kg, machine 30-150 kg, base 15-20 cm square, 500-750 blows/min; suit cohesive and semi-cohesive soils.

Vibratory plate: large base plate vibrated by an engine-driven eccentric weight, low amplitude, high frequency; compacts granular soils and bituminous mixes in thin layers; gives a level finish.

X·7Compare well points and deep wells.
  • Well points: shallow, closely spaced, suction lift limited to about 5-6 m per stage; sands; quick to install
  • Deep wells: 150-500 mm dia, submersible pumps at the bottom push water up, no suction limit; large drawdowns (20-30 m+) in thick permeable strata; widely spaced; costlier per well
